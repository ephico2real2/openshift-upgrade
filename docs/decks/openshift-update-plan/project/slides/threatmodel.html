Threat model: the platform starts every update

Platform calls, four Red Hat hosts

The Cluster Version Operator, CRI-O on the nodes and OLM make every request: api.openshift.com, mirror.openshift.com, quay.io and its CDN, registry.redhat.io. All HTTPS on TCP 443 through the enterprise proxy, which allows only those hosts (C3); the firewall lets only the proxy out (C1).

Application pods have no way out

The platform gives them no proxy settings (C2) and they are stopped at the firewall (C1). A team that sets a proxy in its own deployment does so by choice, under existing policy.

C1 to C7 are Red Hat's; C8 is ours

Each control is quoted from and linked to the OpenShift 4.18 documentation. C8 is our Kyverno admission policy: images only from Red Hat's and our internal registries.

Read the figure top to bottom: Red Hat's hosts on the internet, our enterprise network with the proxy and the firewall, the OpenShift cluster with the platform on the left and application namespaces on the right, and the cluster admin at the bottom, who only picks the target. The solid arrows are the platform's flows, every one on TCP 443 through the proxy. The dashed arrow from the application pods is the one thing that is blocked. Controls C1 to C7 each cite a Red Hat document; C8, the admission policy, is our own and is labelled as such. This is the document the platform owner takes the change to security on; the one condition is that applications cannot use the proxy by default, which tests 6 and 8 prove. The SMEs and the Product Owner shape the direction of this platform offering, and the proxy approach is the SME's recommendation: a sustainable way to update as we grow, Red Hat's supported way on OpenShift 4.x, with Ansible automating the upgrade either way and the manual mirror eliminated.
