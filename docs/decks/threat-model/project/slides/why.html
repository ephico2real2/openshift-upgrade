Why the proxy

A sustainable way to update as we grow

Not two-headed DNS. The disconnected setup came from OpenShift 3.x, where the proxy setup was a mess. On 4.x the proxy is Red Hat's supported way for enterprise customers to take fixes as they ship, ready for the growth that follows (OpenShift 5.x, for example).

Ansible automates the upgrade either way, under the proxy too: the hops, the update service, the registry settings, catalog gating. What goes is the manual mirror.

The SMEs and the Product Owner shape the direction of this platform offering; the proxy approach is the SME's professional recommendation, from experience and modern best practice. The Ansible mirror is a lot of work to maintain, and that time is better spent on the update process itself, through the proxy. Ansible automates the upgrade under both options, the retrofit and the update runs included under the proxy: the hops themselves, the update service, the registry configuration and catalog gating through GitOps become playbooks; the difference is the manual mirror, which the proxy approach eliminates. With the proxy enabled on 4.18 first, the hops to 4.20 and 4.22 need no sync at all. The platform owner decides, and takes the change to security on this threat model. The one condition is that consumers and applications cannot use the proxy by default: the platform gives them no proxy settings (C2) and they have no route out (C1), proven by Verify tests 6 and 8. With that met, we are good. The mirror approach, automated, is the fallback only; Red Hat Quay on-prem would only change its registry product, not a third option.
